Power Outage Analysis

Exploring major U.S. power outages from 2000–2016
and what makes them last longer.

Fei (Yinfei) Liang • Alina (Yueming) Gao

Introduction

This project analyzes a dataset of major power outages in the United States from 2000 to 2016. The dataset contains 1,534 outage events with 56 variables, including outage duration, cause category, climate region, and the number of customers affected.

Our research question is: Does the cause of a power outage (specifically severe weather versus other causes) significantly affect how long the outage lasts?

Power outages affect millions of people every year. When the power goes out, hospitals lose electricity, food spoils, and people lose heat or air conditioning. Knowing what causes the longest outages can help power companies prepare better and fix problems faster. Since storms and extreme weather are becoming more common, it is especially important to understand whether weather-related outages tend to last longer than outages from other causes.

Assessment of Missingness

We believe DEMAND.LOSS.MW is likely NMAR. Utility companies may intentionally leave this field blank when the demand loss was caused by negligence or operational error, to limit liability exposure. The value is more likely missing because of what the value itself is, not because of other recorded columns. Additional data such as regulatory incident reports or insurance filings could help explain the missingness, potentially making it MAR.

We tested whether the missingness of DEMAND.LOSS.MW depends on CAUSE.CATEGORY (TVD=0.179, p<0.001) and OUTAGE.DURATION (difference=128.8 minutes, p=0.660). The missingness does depend on cause category, suggesting certain types of outages are more likely to have demand loss data missing. It does not depend on outage duration, suggesting the length of the outage does not influence whether this value was recorded.

Test 1 — CAUSE.CATEGORY (TVD = 0.179, p < 0.001): The histogram below shows the empirical null distribution of the Total Variation Distance (TVD) between the cause-category distributions of rows where DEMAND.LOSS.MW is missing versus not missing. Each bar represents how often a particular TVD arose by chance under random shuffling. The observed TVD of 0.179 (red dashed line) falls far to the right of the null distribution — nearly no permutation produced a TVD this large — giving a p-value close to zero. This means the missingness of DEMAND.LOSS.MW is not independent of cause category: certain outage types (e.g., intentional attacks) are far more likely to have the demand-loss field left blank, suggesting the data are MAR with respect to cause.

Test 2 — OUTAGE.DURATION (|Diff in Means| = 128.8 min, p = 0.660): The histogram below shows the null distribution of the absolute difference in mean outage duration between rows where DEMAND.LOSS.MW is missing versus present. The observed difference of 128.8 minutes (red dashed line) sits well inside the bulk of the null distribution — roughly 66% of random shuffles produced a larger gap — giving a p-value of 0.660. We fail to reject the null hypothesis, meaning the missingness of DEMAND.LOSS.MW does not depend on outage duration. Whether an outage lasted a short or a long time has no statistically detectable relationship with whether the demand-loss figure was recorded.

Hypothesis Testing

Null Hypothesis: The distribution of outage duration for severe weather events is the same as for non-severe weather events. Any observed difference is due to random chance.

Alternative Hypothesis: Power outages caused by severe weather tend to last longer on average than those caused by other factors.

Test Statistic: Difference in mean outage duration (severe weather minus all other causes). This is appropriate because we are comparing a numerical variable across two groups and care about the direction of the difference.

Significance Level: 0.05  |  P-value: <0.001

Since the p-value is below our significance level, we reject the null hypothesis. This suggests that severe weather outages tend to last significantly longer than outages from other causes. However, since this is an observational study and not a randomized controlled trial, we cannot conclude that severe weather directly causes longer outages.

Framing a Prediction Problem

We build a multiclass classifier to predict the cause of a power outage (CAUSE.CATEGORY) using four features that are all observable before an official cause is determined. Climate region and anomaly level capture the environmental conditions surrounding the outage. Month reflects seasonal patterns that influence the likelihood of certain causes, such as severe weather being more common in summer. Number of customers affected is recorded early in incident response and gives a sense of the outage's scale, which tends to differ across causes. Together, these features represent information a utility company would realistically have access to at the start of an incident, before any formal cause classification has been made.

Baseline Model

Our baseline model is a Decision Tree classifier (max_depth=2) implemented as a single sklearn Pipeline. It uses two features:

FeatureTypeEncoding
CLIMATE.REGIONNominal (9 categories)OneHotEncoder — avoids false ordinal relationships
MONTHOrdinal (1–12)Passed through as numeric — natural ordering is meaningful

The baseline accuracy was 50.66%. This is slightly above majority-class guessing (~49% for severe weather), showing that climate region and month provide a small signal. However, the model is still too simple and needs more features to be useful.

Final Model

We switched to a RandomForestClassifier and added three features on top of the baseline. ANOMALY.LEVEL was added because climate anomaly severity is directly tied to how likely an outage is caused by weather. Extreme anomaly values signal unusual atmospheric conditions that cause severe weather events. OUTAGE.DURATION was added because the cause of an outage fundamentally shapes how long recovery takes. Severe weather damages widespread infrastructure while intentional attacks tend to be more targeted and resolved faster. CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED was added because weather events typically knock out power across large regions affecting many customers at once, while causes like equipment failure tend to be more localized. We imputed missing values in CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED with the median rather than dropping rows, to preserve as much training data as possible. We chose RandomForestClassifier over the baseline Decision Tree because it builds many trees and averages their predictions, reducing overfitting. We tuned n_estimators (50, 100, 200) and max_depth (5, 10, None) using GridSearchCV with 5-fold cross-validation. The best parameters were n_estimators=100 and max_depth=None. The final model achieved 70.39% accuracy compared to the baseline's 50.66%, a improvement of over 19 percentage points.

Fairness Analysis

Group X (West): Outages in western climate regions (West, Northwest, Southwest)
Group Y (Non-West): Outages in all other climate regions

Null Hypothesis: Our model is fair. Its accuracy for western and non-western outages is roughly the same, and any observed difference is due to random chance.

Alternative Hypothesis: Our model is unfair. Its accuracy is lower for western outages than for non-western outages.

Test Statistic: Difference in accuracy (West minus Non-West)  |  Significance Level: 0.05

The observed difference was -0.1771 (West: 0.604167, Non-West: 0.781250). The p-value was 0.0070, which is below 0.05, so we reject the null hypothesis. Our model performs significantly worse on western outages, likely because the western US has a distinct outage profile (wildfires, droughts) that is underrepresented in the training data.

Conclusion

Through this project, we investigated what drives the severity of major power outages in the United States. Our hypothesis test confirmed that severe weather outages last significantly longer than outages from other causes, with an observed difference of 2,537.81 minutes on average and a p-value below 0.001. This suggests that the cause of an outage is strongly associated with how long it lasts.

We also built a multiclass classifier to predict the cause of an outage from observable features. Our baseline Decision Tree model achieved 50.66% accuracy using only climate region and month. By switching to a Random Forest and adding features like anomaly level, outage duration, and customers affected, we improved accuracy to 70.39%, demonstrating that these variables carry meaningful signal about what causes an outage.

Finally, our fairness analysis revealed that the model performs significantly worse on western outages than non-western ones, with a p-value of 0.007. This suggests that western US outages have a distinct profile that is underrepresented in the training data, pointing to a direction for future improvement.
